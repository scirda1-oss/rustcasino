Every skin deposit on a Rust gambling site is really a deposit of **market value** — the site credits you based on what your item is worth. So understanding what drives that value isn't trivia; it's how you judge whether a conversion rate is fair, and whether the skin in your inventory is even the kind a site will accept. Here's where Rust skins come from, what actually moves their prices, and the mechanics that quietly shape every deposit.

## Rust's market works differently from CS2

If you've come from CS2, drop those instincts. Rust skins have **no float/wear values** and **no stamped rarity grades** on each item. There's no "Factory New vs Battle-Scarred" and no colour-coded tier deciding a price. Value is set almost entirely by two things: **scarcity and demand** at a given moment. That makes the market simpler to understand but more volatile — a skin is worth what someone will pay for it today, full stop.

## Where Rust skins actually come from

Supply is the half of the equation most players never look at, and in Rust it comes from a small number of taps:

- **The Item Store.** Facepunch sells a rotating selection of skins through Rust's Steam Item Store, refreshed weekly around the game's Thursday update. Each skin is on sale for a limited window, then rotates out. While it's on sale, supply is effectively unlimited — anyone can buy one at the sticker price.
- **The Steam Workshop.** Most store skins are made by community artists who submit designs to Rust's Workshop; Facepunch picks which ones get sold. That's why the catalogue is huge and stylistically all over the place — and why a skin's *look* carries real weight in a market with no rarity tiers.
- **Twitch Drops.** Facepunch runs periodic drop campaigns where watching partnered streams earns skins. The campaign ends, and so does the supply. Most drops become tradable after Steam's standard holds; some tournament-specific items (Twitch Rivals, for instance) are typically non-marketable and stay locked to the account that earned them — which matters if you were planning to deposit them.
- **The Community Market.** Not a source of *new* skins, but the place all of the above gets resold once it leaves the store. This is where the price you see on a site's deposit screen ultimately comes from.

Once a skin leaves the store or its drop campaign ends, **no more are created**. From that point, every trade, every gambling loss and every abandoned account slowly tightens supply.

## What actually drives value

A handful of durable factors do most of the work:

- **Discontinuation.** Once a skin stops being sold, its supply is fixed. Items that never return to the store tend to hold value best; items Facepunch re-runs get knocked back down toward sticker price.
- **Age.** Older skins from early store rotations often carry a premium simply because no more are being created while new players keep joining and wanting them.
- **Design appeal.** Clean, distinctive designs hold value better than busy or generic ones — taste matters when there's no rarity label to lean on.
- **Special-drop scarcity.** Skins from past Twitch drop campaigns have a hard-capped supply, which is why some become sought-after long after the campaign.
- **What the item goes on.** A skin for something everyone uses every wipe (a common weapon, a door, a sleeping bag) has a far deeper pool of buyers than one for a niche item — and depth of demand is what keeps a price from collapsing when a few holders sell.

The common thread is **fixed or shrinking supply meeting ongoing demand.** That's what turns a cosmetic into a store of value.

## The two mechanics that eat into every deposit

Two Steam rules sit underneath all of this, and both show up on the deposit screen:

- **The 15% market fee.** Selling a Rust skin on the Community Market costs a 5% Steam fee plus a 10% game fee. So the "market price" you see is what a *buyer* pays; a seller pockets roughly 85% of it. Gambling sites know this, which is why crediting a deposit at 85–90% of listed price isn't automatically a rip-off — it's close to what you'd net selling it yourself. Materially *below* that is the spread you're actually paying.
- **The 7-day hold.** Skins bought on the Community Market can't be traded or re-listed for a week. If you buy a skin specifically to deposit it, you're waiting seven days before a site can take it. Some sites also won't accept items that are still in a hold, so plan around it. ## The bottom line

Rust skin value is pure supply and demand: scarce, discontinued, aged or well-designed items hold up; common ones don't. Supply comes from a few known taps — the weekly Item Store, workshop picks, Twitch drops — and closes the moment a skin leaves them. Before you convert any skin into site balance, know roughly what it's worth on the open market and what you'd actually net after Steam's cut — because the moment it becomes "balance," you've accepted whatever rate the site offered.
