You signed in with Steam, deposited a few skins, and never typed your real name once. Then you go to cash out a decent balance — and the site asks for a photo of your ID. For a lot of players that's the moment gambling stops feeling anonymous. Here's what's actually going on, when to expect it, how to get through it in one pass, and — just as important — how to avoid sending your passport to someone who isn't the site at all.

## What KYC means, and what it's actually for

KYC stands for **Know Your Customer** — identity verification. In practice a site asks you to prove who you are, usually with a government ID, and sometimes where you live and that a payment method is yours. It's the same process banks and regulated casinos run, and it serves a handful of specific purposes:

- **Age.** Confirming you're over 18 (or the local limit).
- **Fraud and stolen accounts.** Making sure a cash-out goes to the account's real owner, not to someone who phished it — the [API-key scam](/news/steam-api-key-phishing-rust-gambling) is exactly the kind of thing this blocks.
- **Payment ownership.** Confirming a card, wallet or bank account belongs to you, so stolen payment details can't be laundered through the site.
- **Licence and location.** Licensed operators have to show players are inside the territory their licence covers and aren't on a self-exclusion list.
- **Anti-money-laundering.** For licensed sites, a legal obligation above certain amounts; for unlicensed ones, mostly a fraud filter.

That last line is the key to reading a site's behaviour: a **licensed** operator applies checks more readily and more consistently. That's not a bad sign — it usually means the opposite.

## When it triggers

Most Rust sites don't ask for KYC just to **play** — you can log in and bet without it. It tends to kick in on the **withdrawal** side, and the common triggers are predictable:

- **Your first withdrawal**, on some sites, regardless of size.
- **Crossing an amount threshold** — a single large cash-out, or cumulative withdrawals over a period. Thresholds vary by operator and are usually in the terms.
- **Cashing out to crypto or fiat** rather than skins. Skin withdrawals are a Steam trade; money leaving the platform is where AML and payment-ownership checks concentrate. (Our [cash-out guide](/news/cash-out-rust-winnings-real-money) covers the paths.)
- **A method mismatch** — depositing one way and withdrawing another, or to a wallet or account the site hasn't seen before.
- **Location or device signals** — a VPN, logins from a new country, or patterns that look like an account takeover.
- **Bonus abuse flags** — multiple accounts, or a bonus being cleared in a way the site considers suspicious.

If you plan to build and cash out a real balance, assume at least one of these will apply, and read the withdrawal and KYC section of the terms **before** you deposit, not after you win.

## What they'll ask for — and why documents get rejected

Requests usually fall into three groups:

- **Proof of identity:** passport, driver's licence or national ID card. Some sites add a selfie holding the document.
- **Proof of address:** a utility bill or bank statement, typically dated within the last three months.
- **Proof of payment ownership:** a statement or screenshot showing the card, bank account or wallet is yours.

Most delays aren't the site stalling — they're a rejected upload. The usual reasons: a **blurry, cropped or glare-covered** photo; an **expired** document; an **edited** file (even just blacking out a field the site wants); or a **name mismatch** between your account details and the ID. Photograph the whole document flat, in good light, all four corners visible, and make sure the name on the site matches the name on the ID exactly. One clean submission beats three sloppy ones.

## Legitimate check or withholding tactic?

Here's the line worth understanding. A site asking for standard KYC once, on a large withdrawal, is normal. A site that keeps **moving the goalposts** — demanding more and more documents, each time you clear the last, or inventing new "security reviews" with no end date — is using verification as a **withholding tactic**. Legitimate KYC has a defined list and an end; a stall doesn't. ## Don't send your ID to the wrong people

KYC is also a scam vector, and this is the part most guides skip. Scammers know players expect to be asked for documents, so they ask first:

- **Real support never DMs you first** on Discord or Steam. An account with a staff name and avatar messaging you to "complete verification" is an impersonator — especially if it appears right after you've posted a complaint somewhere public.
- **Upload documents only inside your account on the site's real domain** — the verification page in your account settings. Never through a link in a DM, an email, or a "secure portal" you were sent.
- **No fee ever unlocks a withdrawal.** A "verification deposit," "tax" or "release fee" to get your winnings out is a scam, full stop.
- **Never share login codes or passwords** with anyone claiming to be support.

If you've already sent ID to the wrong place: change your passwords, enable two-factor everywhere, watch for new accounts opened in your name, and consider an identity-theft report and a credit freeze with your national authority. Documents, unlike a lost coinflip, can be reused against you for years.

## A quick checklist

- **Read the withdrawal + KYC section** of a site's terms before you deposit.
- **Have a valid, unexpired ID ready** if you intend to cash out anything sizeable, and keep your account name matching it.
- **Expect skin withdrawals to be quicker** than crypto or fiat cash-outs.
- **Submit once, cleanly** — flat, lit, uncropped, unedited.
- **Only ever upload inside your account on the real domain.** Treat any other request as phishing.
- **Treat endless document requests as a red flag**, not routine.
