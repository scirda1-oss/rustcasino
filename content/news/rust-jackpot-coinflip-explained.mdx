Jackpot and coinflip feel different from crash or the upgrader: you're not playing a curve or a multiplier, you're up against **other players**. That makes them feel like fair, house-free contests. They aren't — the site still takes a cut, and on one of these games that cut can be the biggest edge on the whole platform. Here's how both work, how to check a draw, and the one piece of arithmetic that tells you what each game actually costs.

## Jackpot: your share is your odds

In jackpot, multiple players deposit skins into a shared **pot**. Under the hood, the site converts every deposit into **tickets** in proportion to its value — a common convention is one ticket per cent, so a $10 skin is 1,000 tickets. When the timer ends, one ticket number is drawn and whoever holds it takes the whole pot, minus the site's cut.

That makes your odds simple arithmetic: **your value divided by the total pot**. Put in 10% of the pot and you have roughly a 10% chance. The odds are transparent, which is the appeal. But the maths is unforgiving in both directions: throw a small amount into a big pot and you're a long shot; dominate a pot to get good odds and you're risking a lot to win comparatively little on top.

## Coinflip: near-even, but not quite

Coinflip is the simple one: **1v1**, two players stake similar values, a virtual flip decides, and the winner takes both stakes minus the site's cut. With equal stakes the base odds are a clean 50/50. If the stakes aren't equal — most sites allow a small tolerance so flips fill faster — your chance is, again, **your stake divided by the total**, so the bigger side is slightly favoured. On any single flip it's close to even; across a session, the edge shows up.

## How the draw is decided — and how to check it

Both games are usually **provably fair**, and the mechanism is the same commit-reveal idea used across skin sites:

- **Before the round**, the site publishes a **hash** of a secret server seed. The hash can't be reversed, but it locks the seed in — change the seed later and the hash won't match.
- **After the round**, the seed is revealed. Combining it with the round's other inputs through a fixed formula produces the **winning ticket number** (jackpot) or the side of the flip (coinflip).
- Some operators add a **public seed** the site can't control — typically the hash of a blockchain block that's only produced *after* the players have joined — so the operator can't quietly pick a seed that favours one player. On a 1v1 flip, the result is committed before the second player joins, and either player can recompute it afterwards.

You don't need to do the maths by hand; the site's verifier page does it, and our [step-by-step guide](/blog/how-to-verify-provably-fair) walks through it. What you *should* do is check that the hash shown before the round matches the seed revealed after it — if a site's "provably fair" page can't show you both, read our [fake provably fair red flags](/news/fake-provably-fair-red-flags) before you deposit.

One thing verification doesn't do: it proves the draw wasn't rigged. It says nothing about whether the game is worth playing. That's what the next section is for.

## The rake is where the edge lives — and the one formula to remember

The site keeps a **commission (rake)** off the pot before paying out. That rake *is* the [house edge](/news/house-edge-on-rust-gambling-explained) for these games, and it varies a lot between sites — some keep it modest, some don't. Here's why it's the only number that matters:

- Say you deposit value **v** into a pot of total value **V**, and the rake is **r**.
- Your chance of winning is v/V. If you win, you receive V × (1 − r). If you lose, you lose v.
- Expected result per round: (v/V) × V × (1 − r) − v = **−r × v**.

The pot size cancels out. **On average you lose the rake times whatever you put in — every round, regardless of how big the pot is or what share of it you hold.** A 5% rake costs you 5% of each deposit over time; a 10% rake costs double. The same formula holds for coinflip: with a fee f on the pot, each flip costs you f × your stake on average.

Two things follow from that:

- **"Sniping" a pot doesn't beat the rake.** Jumping in late with a big deposit to grab a high share of a pot changes your *variance* — you win more often, for smaller profit — but your expected loss is still exactly r × v. Nobody has an edge in jackpot except the site.
- **Rake is a headline number, not fine print.** Two sites with identical games can cost you twice as much purely because of commission. ## Streaks are normal — plan for them

Because every flip is independent, losing runs are a matter of *when*, not *if*. The probability of losing N even coinflips in a row is 0.5ᴺ: five in a row is about 3%, seven about 0.8%. Those sound rare, but across a hundred flips they're expected, not unlucky. Size stakes so a seven-flip losing streak is survivable and uninteresting — our [bankroll basics](/news/rust-gambling-bankroll-basics) covers how. And remember no pot is "owed" to you; a losing streak isn't "due" to end.
