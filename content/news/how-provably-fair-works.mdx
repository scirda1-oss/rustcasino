"Provably fair" gets thrown around a lot, but the mechanism underneath is genuinely elegant once you see it — a **commit-reveal trick** that swaps "trust the operator" for "check the math." Here's how it actually works, without the jargon.

## The problem it solves

On an ordinary site, you're trusting the operator not to quietly rig the outcome *after* seeing your bet. Provably fair removes that trust: it lets you prove a result was **locked in before you played**. It does this with three ingredients:

- **Server seed** — the site's secret random value.
- **Client seed** — your value, which you can set or change.
- **Nonce** — a counter that ticks up by one with each bet on the same seed pair, so every bet is unique without needing a fresh server seed each time.

## Step 1: the commitment (why the hash comes first)

Before you bet, the site shows you the **hash** of its server seed — typically a SHA-256 fingerprint — **not the seed itself**. A cryptographic hash is one-way: you can't work backwards from the fingerprint to the seed, but the site **can't change the seed later** without the fingerprint no longer matching. That published hash is the lock — a promise the site can't quietly break.

## Step 2: producing the result

When you place a bet, the three inputs — **server seed + your client seed + nonce** — are run through a keyed hash (an HMAC), producing a long hexadecimal string. That string is then converted, deterministically, into the game outcome: a crash point, a wheel slot, a roll. Same inputs always give the same output, so the result isn't a live decision — it's a calculation.

## Step 3: the reveal and the check

After the round, the site **reveals the actual server seed**. Now you do two checks:

- **Hash the revealed seed yourself** and confirm it matches the fingerprint shown *before* you bet. That proves the seed wasn't swapped.
- **Re-run the same HMAC** with that seed, your client seed, and the nonce, and confirm you get the **same outcome**. That proves the result wasn't fabricated.

If both match, the round was honest. The [step-by-step walkthrough](/blog/how-to-verify-provably-fair) shows exactly where to click.

## Why your client seed matters

Because **you** control the client seed, the site couldn't have pre-computed a result rigged against you — it committed to its own seed without knowing yours. That's the other half of the guarantee, and it's why a system that **won't let you set a client seed** isn't really provably fair.

## What it still doesn't prove

Here's the honest limit: provably fair proves the **draw was honest** — not that the odds are generous, and not that the site will actually pay your withdrawal. The [house edge](/house-edge-on-rust-gambling-explained) applies to a perfectly fair game too. And a hollow "provably fair" badge with no reveal and no verifier proves nothing at all ([spotting a fake one](/fake-provably-fair-red-flags)).

## Bottom line

Provably fair is a commit-reveal trick: **hash first to lock, reveal after to prove.** It's clever, verifiable, and worth insisting on — just remember it certifies the honesty of each draw, not that you'll win or get paid.
