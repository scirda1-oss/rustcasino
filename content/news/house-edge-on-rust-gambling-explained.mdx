A lot of Rust players read "provably fair" and assume it means the odds are even. It doesn't. Provably fair proves a *specific result* wasn't tampered with after you bet — it says nothing about whether the game was tilted in the site's favour to begin with. That tilt is the **house edge**, and understanding it — including where it physically sits in each game — is the difference between playing with your eyes open and being surprised when a balance drains.

## What house edge actually is

House edge is the built-in mathematical advantage a game gives the operator: the share of each bet that, on average, the site keeps over the long run. Its mirror image is **RTP** (return to player). A game with a 5% house edge has a 95% RTP — over a very large number of bets it pays back around 95% of what's wagered.

- **House edge = 1 − RTP**
- **Expected loss per bet = edge × stake**

The key phrase is **over the long run**. On any single spin, flip or case you can win big or lose everything — that's variance. But the more you play, the more your results grind toward the edge. It isn't a glitch or a rigged night; it's the design working as intended.

## Provably fair ≠ no edge

This is the part people miss. A [provably fair](/blog/how-to-verify-provably-fair) system lets you verify that the server didn't change the outcome after seeing your bet. That's genuinely worth having. But a game can be 100% provably fair **and** carry a hefty house edge at the same time — the two are unrelated. The cryptography proves the dice weren't swapped; it doesn't make the dice fair-weighted. Verify fairness, yes, but don't read it as "the odds are in my favour."

## Where the edge hides, game by game

The edge is set **per game**, not per site, and it's built in differently each time. Once you know where to look, you can compute it yourself from numbers the site publishes:

- **Roulette — the green slot.** The common layout is 15 slots: 7 red, 7 black, 1 green. Red or black pays 2x, green pays 14x. Bet on red: you win 7 times in 15 and get 2x, so you're returned 14/15 of your stakes — a **6.67% edge**. Green pays 14x on a 1-in-15 chance: also 14/15, also 6.67%. The green slot *is* the edge; every colour pays as if it didn't exist. (Full breakdown in our [roulette explainer](/news/rust-roulette-game-explained).)
- **Crash — the formula.** In a typical implementation the chance the multiplier reaches at least *m* is **RTP ÷ m**. So at 99% RTP, 2x hits 49.5% of the time, 10x hits 9.9%. Your expected return at *any* cash-out target is the same: RTP − 1. Cashing out early or late changes your variance, never your edge. Some sites express the edge as an instant-bust rate (round ends at 1.00x), others fold it into the formula — either way it's the published RTP you need. ([Crash explainer](/news/rust-crash-game-explained).)
- **Upgrader — the win chance.** Your chance to upgrade a $10 skin into a $20 one isn't 50%; it's 50% × RTP. The gap between "fair odds" and the odds shown is the edge, printed on screen before every click. ([Upgrader explainer](/news/rust-upgrader-game-explained).)
- **Cases — the price.** Sum each item's drop probability × market value, divide by the case price: that's the RTP. The edge varies wildly between cases on the same site, which is why a site's "overall" figure tells you nothing about the case in front of you. ([Case battles](/news/rust-case-battles-explained).)
- **Jackpot and coinflip — the rake.** The site takes a commission off the pot. Your expected loss per round is rake × your deposit, no matter how big the pot or your share of it. ([Jackpot & coinflip](/news/rust-jackpot-coinflip-explained).)

Notice the pattern: in every case the edge is a **published or derivable number**. Sites that make it impossible to derive are telling you something.

## What a session actually costs

Expected loss is simply **edge × average stake × number of bets**. The number of bets is the part players underestimate, because skin games are fast:

- 300 roulette spins at $1 on a 6.67% edge: about **$20** expected loss.
- 300 crash rounds at $1 on a 1% edge: about **$3**.
- 50 case openings at $5 on a 20% edge: about **$50**.

Same hour, same "small bets," radically different cost — driven by edge and speed, not by luck. This is also why [rakeback](/news/free-coins-rains-rakeback-vip-explained) is the only promo worth weighting: returning 10% of a 6.67% edge drops it to 6% — real, but it never gets to zero.

## Variance: why the edge is invisible until it isn't

Over a small number of bets, luck dominates the edge completely — which is exactly why the edge is easy to disbelieve. Over a large number, the edge dominates luck: your expected loss grows in proportion to the number of bets, while the random swings grow only with its square root. Double the bets and the edge doubles, but the noise grows by only about 1.4x. Keep going and the result converges on the maths.

Different games deliver that edge differently:

- **Low-variance games** (even-money style bets) bleed you slowly and steadily.
- **High-variance games** (jackpots, big-multiplier cases, late crash cash-outs) deliver rare big wins and frequent losses — same edge, far wilder path, and far more likely to flatter you early.

Neither beats the maths. Variance just changes *how* the edge reaches you — a slow bleed versus a rollercoaster.

## What to actually do with this

- **Treat every session as paid entertainment**, not an income plan. The edge guarantees the site's advantage over time, not yours.
- **Find the edge before you play** — from the odds table, the RTP figure, the roulette layout or the rake. If you can't derive it, don't play it.
- **Weigh edge × speed**, not just edge. A low-edge game played very fast can cost more than a high-edge one played slowly.
- **Use provably fair for what it's for** — confirming a result wasn't altered — not as proof the game is generous.
- **Set a loss limit before you start**, because variance can flatter you early and the edge collects later.
