Are skins and loot boxes "gambling" in the eyes of the law? It's a fair question, and the honest answer is: it depends heavily on where you are and exactly what mechanic you mean. Regulators have spent years on the *in-game loot box* question, while *third-party skin gambling* has largely stayed a step ahead of the rules. Here's the landscape, kept to what's actually established.

## Two different things regulators look at

- **Loot boxes** are the in-game paid randomised rewards sold by the game itself.
- **Skin gambling** happens on **third-party sites** that let you wager skins on games of chance — separate businesses built on the item economy, not the game publisher.

Most legal attention has landed on the first; the second mostly operates in an unregulated grey zone.

## What regulators have actually done

Two well-documented examples show how differently jurisdictions treat this:

- **Belgium** ruled back in 2018 that **paid loot boxes fall under its gambling law**, and takes a notably strict line — its authorities have argued that even items with value *to the player* can qualify, not just tradeable ones.
- The **Netherlands** took a more winding path: its gambling regulator fined EA **€10 million in 2020** over FIFA Ultimate Team packs, arguing the random-reward-with-tradeable-outcomes mechanic fell under Dutch gambling law. That fine was **overturned on appeal in 2022** by the country's highest administrative court, which narrowed how the law applied to mechanics tied to a game-of-skill product.

The takeaway isn't a single global rule — it's that **jurisdictions disagree**, the law is still catching up, and outcomes hinge on fine details like whether a reward can be cashed out.

## Where skin gambling sits

Third-party Rust skin gambling sites are the part with the *least* regulatory coverage. In most places they operate without a gambling licence and without oversight, which is why — as we've covered in [whether it's against Steam's rules](/is-rust-skin-gambling-against-steam-rules) — there's typically no regulator to complain to and no consumer-protection backstop if something goes wrong.

## What this means for you

- **Legality varies by country.** Whether using these sites is legal where *you* live is on you to check; some jurisdictions restrict or prohibit it entirely.
- **Unregulated means unprotected.** No licence usually means none of the player protections a regulated casino must provide — deposit limits, dispute resolution, audited fairness.
- **The rules are moving.** Scrutiny of loot boxes and skin gambling has trended toward *more* regulation over time, not less, so today's grey area may not stay grey.

## Bottom line

Loot boxes have been dragged, unevenly, toward gambling law in some countries; third-party skin gambling largely hasn't been reached yet. For you, the practical consequence is simple: treat these sites as **unregulated and unprotected**, know your own local law, and don't expect any authority to have your back if a site misbehaves.
